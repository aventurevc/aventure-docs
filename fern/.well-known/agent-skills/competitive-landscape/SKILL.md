# Competitive Landscape

The deliverable maps the whole field the subject competes in, so a reader sees who else wins the same buyers, how the field is moving, and where the subject stands. A competitor analysis goes deep on a few rivals; this report goes wide.

## Inputs

- The subject: an aVenture URL or a name. The URL wins when both appear.
- Segment or region (optional): when given, it is the landscape's boundary.
- Context (optional): the reader's purpose, period, or players of interest.

## 1. Pin the Subject

1. Read the subject's aVenture record before any web search: description, headquarters, founding year, funding rounds, people, news, and research.
2. Name the exact entity covered. Parents, subsidiaries, brands, and spin-offs are distinct: Nabisco is a Mondelez brand, not a company; Kellogg Company split in October 2023 into Kellanova and the spun-off WK Kellogg Co. Map the landscape of the business unit that actually sells to the buyer.
3. When two records fit the name, stop and report both candidates with the fact that separates them.
4. An investment firm's landscape is the set of investors competing for the same deals: same stage, sector, geography, and check size.

## 2. Set the Boundary

State in one sentence: the buyer, the job they pay for, and the geography. Every player in the report must sell to that buyer for that job, or be a named substitute for it.

## 3. Find the Players

1. Start from aVenture's similar-company results and the subject's own comparisons, then search the web for the category name, buyer reviews, analyst category lists, and "alternatives to" pages.
2. Verify each candidate against the boundary from its own site or documentation before including it. Similar descriptions do not prove the same buyer.
3. Sort players into classes: incumbents, funded challengers, adjacent platforms that bundle the capability, open-source or in-house alternatives, and substitutes that solve the job another way.
4. Aim for eight to twenty players. Name the ones excluded near the boundary and the reason.

## 4. Analyze

- Positioning axes: pick the two dimensions buyers actually trade off in this segment (for example price against depth of integration, or self-serve against enterprise sales), justify them with buyer evidence, and place each player on them in prose or a table.
- Player table: name, founded, headquarters, scale signal (revenue, customers, headcount, or total funding, each with its date), target buyer, pricing model, and the one capability that distinguishes it.
- Movement in the last 24 months: funding rounds with amounts and lead investors, acquisitions with announcement and completion dates, shutdowns, launches, and price changes.
- Structure: concentrated or fragmented, consolidating or splintering, with the evidence.
- Open positions: buyer needs no player serves well, supported by reviews, pricing gaps, or unmet requirements in public RFPs or forums.
- The subject's position: its class, its place on the axes, and the players closest to it.

## 5. Write the Report

Order:
1. Answer: three to five sentences on the field's shape and the subject's place in it.
2. Boundary and player classes.
3. Player table.
4. Positioning on the two axes.
5. Last 24 months of funding, M&A, launches, and exits.
6. Open positions and the subject's position.
7. Gaps: what could not be sourced.

## Evidence Rules

- Source ladder: filings and regulators, then company primary sources (sites, documentation, pricing pages, press releases), then dated reporting from established outlets, then analyst estimates labeled as estimates.
- Every scale figure carries its date. A two-year-old headcount is labeled with its year.
- Keep announcement and completion dates apart; a first close, an announced deal, or a planned product is labeled as such. A company that once acquired another does not prove it still owns it.
- Undisclosed figures are written as not disclosed, never estimated silently.
- Cite each sourced fact where it is used.

## Writing Rules

- Each sentence states a fact, a number, a comparison, or a judgment tied to evidence.
- Never write: "crowded space", "rapidly evolving landscape", "key players", "game-changer", "cutting-edge", "robust", "seamless", "unlock", "leverage", "delve", "it's worth noting", "in conclusion", or a closing summary that repeats the answer.
- Describe a player by what it sells and to whom, not by its slogan.
- Numbers carry currency, unit, period, and an as-of date.
- State uncertainty once, at the claim it affects, with its cause.
