# Market Research

The deliverable is a report that lets an investor or operator decide whether the subject's market is large, growing, and reachable enough to matter. Every figure carries its unit, period, and source; every conclusion traces to a figure.

## Inputs

- The subject: an aVenture URL or a name. The URL wins when both appear.
- Market or segment (optional): when given, it fixes the market boundary; analyze that boundary even when the subject also sells elsewhere, and say so.
- Context (optional): constraints the reader set, such as a geography, a period, or the decision the report informs.

## 1. Pin the Subject

1. Read the subject's aVenture record before any web search: description, headquarters, founding year, funding rounds, people, news, and research. An aVenture URL resolves to its record in one read.
2. Name the exact legal entity the report covers. Parents, subsidiaries, brands, and spin-offs are distinct subjects: Nabisco is a Mondelez brand, not a company; Kellogg Company split in October 2023 into Kellanova and the spun-off WK Kellogg Co, and figures from before the split describe neither one alone. State the structure and the date it took effect when it shapes the numbers.
3. A product subject is analyzed in its own market; its provider is context, not the subject.
4. When two records fit the name, stop and report both candidates with the fact that separates them instead of choosing.

## 2. Define the Market

State the boundary in one sentence before sizing anything: the buyer, the job the buyer pays to get done, the product category, and the geography. A market defined by the subject's own marketing copy ("the AI-native workflow market") is not a boundary; restate it as buyers and budgets.

## 3. Size It

1. Build a bottom-up estimate: number of buyers in the boundary × share who buy the category × annual price. Show each factor with its source.
2. Find a top-down figure from a statistics agency, regulator, public-company filings, or a named research firm. Record the publisher, the year published, the year measured, and the publisher's own market definition.
3. Reconcile the two. When they differ by more than 2×, explain which definition causes the gap and which number the report relies on.
4. Report TAM, SAM, and SOM only when each has a stated basis; SOM is the share the subject could plausibly win given its current distribution, and it needs a stated assumption, not a round percentage.
5. Growth: a historical CAGR from measured years beats a forecast. Label every forecast with its publisher and horizon.

## 4. Analyze

- Segments: split the market along the axis buyers actually choose on (company size, use case, channel, region). Give each segment's size or share where a source measures it.
- Demand drivers and constraints: each one names its mechanism and its evidence (a regulation date, a price change, a measured adoption rate). A driver without evidence is cut.
- Buyers: who signs, from which budget line, through what purchase process, with what switching cost.
- Pricing: published price points and models across the main sellers; note where prices are not public.
- Regulation: rules in force or scheduled that change demand or cost, with dates.
- The subject's position: its revenue or scale where disclosed, its share of the boundary where computable, and which segments it serves.

## 5. Write the Report

Order:
1. Answer: three to five sentences stating the market's size, growth, and what that means for the subject.
2. Market definition.
3. Size and growth: a table of each estimate with method, year, and source, then the reconciliation.
4. Segments, buyers, pricing, drivers and constraints, regulation.
5. The subject's position.
6. What would change this view: the two or three measurable signals that would move the conclusion.
7. Gaps: every figure the report needed and could not source, stated as not disclosed or not found.

## Evidence Rules

- Source ladder: filings and official statistics, then company primary sources (annual reports, pricing pages, documentation, investor materials), then dated reporting from established outlets, then analyst estimates labeled as estimates.
- Keep reported, estimated, and computed values visibly separate. Never estimate a private company's revenue without labeling the method.
- Keep announcement and completion dates apart; an announced acquisition, a first close, or a planned launch is not a completed one.
- Cite each sourced fact where it is used.

## Writing Rules

- Each sentence states a fact, a number, a comparison, or a judgment tied to evidence. Cut any sentence that does none of these.
- Never write: "in today's fast-paced", "rapidly evolving landscape", "poised for growth", "game-changer", "cutting-edge", "robust", "seamless", "unlock", "leverage", "delve", "navigate the", "it's worth noting", "in conclusion", or a closing summary that repeats the answer.
- Numbers carry currency, unit, period, and an as-of date. Round only after computing.
- State uncertainty once, at the claim it affects, with its cause.
- No generic advice ("focus on the customer"). A recommendation names the action, the evidence for it, and the metric that would show it worked.
- Use a table only for a comparison of three or more rows.
